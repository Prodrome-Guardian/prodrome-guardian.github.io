The gap

Your ward already produces this signal. Nothing reads it between rounds.

Between 2 and 3 million older adults develop delirium in hospital each year in the US, accounting for more than 17.5 million inpatient days.2 Detection still depends on intermittent bedside assessment, so hypoactive delirium in a quiet patient at 03:00 goes unnoticed until the morning round. The agitated patient gets found. The one lying still does not.

We start where the evidence is strongest and the economics are clearest: the post-surgical ward. Postoperative delirium is reported in 5–50% of older surgical patients depending on procedure and cohort,1 around 10% after major elective surgery and higher after cardiac and hip fracture repair.

Evidence

Across more than thirty studies, the delirious brain shows the same pattern: relative delta power rises, alpha power falls, and the theta/alpha ratio increases as faster rhythms give way to slower ones.4 Unlike a bedside score, that change is continuous and does not need the patient awake and cooperative.

Upper bound

100% sensitivity, 96% specificity

Van der Kooi et al. measured relative delta power at a single frontal–parietal derivation (F8–Pz), reaching AUC 0.99.5 The sample was small: 56 patients, 28 with delirium against 28 matched controls.

Conservative bound

AUROC 0.75 (95% CI 0.69–0.82)

Numan et al. tested the same measure prospectively across several Dutch centres in 159 postoperative patients aged 60 and over.6 A small matched sample flatters any classifier, so this is the figure we plan against.

Our position

Screening, not diagnosing

Prodrome Guardian raises the question earlier and routes it to a human. Diagnosis stays a clinical judgement made at the bedside, by your team.

How it works

Neither stream is sufficient alone. NEWS2 catches physiological deterioration but is blind to cognition. EEG catches cortical slowing but says nothing about sepsis or haemorrhage. Prodrome Guardian scores both and fuses them.

Neuro stream

Continuous EEG band power

Four channels at standard 10–20 positions, decomposed into five frequency bands every second, then classified into a discrete brain state.

  • Channels — O1, O2, T3, T4
  • Bands — δ 0.5–4 · θ 4–8 · α 8–13 · β 13–30 · γ 30–100 Hz
  • States — alert · relaxed · drowsy · sleeping · confused · distressed
Physiological stream

NEWS2, computed continuously

The early warning score your wards already read, recalculated every five seconds instead of every four hours, with the per-parameter breakdown kept intact.

  • HR · SpO₂ · temperature · respiratory rate
  • Systolic / diastolic BP · HRV (SDNN)
  • Five of seven NEWS2 parameters implemented today
Fusion

Composite recovery score

A single 0–100 figure combining physiology, cortical state, day-over-day trajectory and patient-reported check-ins, designed to be glanceable at handover.

  • Vitals component — NEWS2-weighted
  • Brain component — band ratios, hemispheric symmetry
  • Trajectory — deviation from expected recovery day
  • Engagement — symptom check-in adherence

Most monitoring programmes die of alert fatigue. Nothing reaches a clinician until it has survived two quieter tiers, and transient states are held against a sustained-duration threshold instead of firing on one sample.

L0

Patient

In-app prompt only. Covers self-resolving states such as brief restlessness or a single out-of-range reading. Never leaves the device.

L1

Caregiver

Push notification to the nominated family member or carer. Used for sustained but non-urgent change, and for missed check-ins.

L2

Clinician

Dashboard alert to the care team. Requires a sustained qualifying state: a confused classification held past the duration threshold, or a NEWS2 trigger.

L3

Emergency

All contacts plus emergency services, with GPS location attached. Also reachable through a press-and-hold control that cannot fire by accident.

The top rung is the one people ask to see. The control takes two seconds of deliberate contact, so a sleeve or a dropped handset does nothing. What goes out is more than a call for help: the alert carries the patient’s vitals, NEWS2, brain state and location, so whoever answers already knows what they are walking into. The patient can stand it down with one tap.

Who it is for

The problem is the same in each: a patient whose state can change without anyone noticing, on a ward where assessment is intermittent. What differs is the evidence behind each population, so we sequence them rather than claiming all three at once.

Post-surgical wards

Pilot now

We start here. The published EEG evidence is strongest, the at-risk window is well defined, and length of stay and cost per case are both measurable inside one admission.

Older adults

Next

Delirium in hospitalised older adults extends well beyond surgery, and hypoactive presentations are the ones most often missed. The same instrument applies. The open problem is dementia, where telling baseline cognitive impairment apart from acute change is hard.

Mental health units

Exploratory

Sleep architecture and cortical state are plausible early indicators of relapse, and the same hardware already captures both. We hold this as a hypothesis. We have not sourced evidence for it, and we would not pitch it as validated.

Design principle

Patient-held by default.

The patient sees their own signal first and controls who else receives it. We treat this as a clinical requirement. Monitoring that a person cannot see or influence is surveillance, and in a mental health setting it damages the therapeutic relationship faster than it detects anything. Every escalation tier above the patient stays visible to the patient.

The ask

We are looking for one ward willing to test a hypothesis.

We are not asking you to buy anything. We want a short observational pilot on a post-surgical unit, running alongside your existing assessment protocol, with your team judging whether the neuro stream surfaces anything the four-hourly round missed.

  • An observational deployment on a defined post-surgical cohort, with no change to standard care
  • Continuous EEG plus vitals capture, scored against your existing delirium assessment as ground truth
  • A shared read of where the model agreed, where it disagreed, and where it produced noise
  • Full access to the raw data and the scoring logic. No part of the algorithm is hidden from you
  • If you run a geriatric or mental health unit, we would still like the conversation. You would be shaping what comes after the first cohort rather than buying it
